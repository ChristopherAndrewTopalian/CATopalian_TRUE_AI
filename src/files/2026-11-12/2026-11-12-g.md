Let's look at the NOT gate and where it fits into your 56-node matrix.

The NOT gate is unique because it is a **unary operation**—it only takes one input (A) instead of two (A and B). This is why it doesn't fit neatly onto the perimeter of your 16 binary logic gates, which all require two inputs to function.

However, the NOT operation is actually already baked directly into the geometry of your system through the concept of the **"True Voids" or "Opposites."**

When you look at the 16 boolean functions, they exist in perfect pairs of opposites (where every bit in the truth table is inverted). For example:

* AND (0001) is the exact opposite of NAND (1110).
* OR (0111) is the exact opposite of NOR (1000).
* XOR (0110) is the exact opposite of XNOR (1001).

In the architecture of a logic circuit, if you want to turn an AND gate into a NAND gate, you simply place a NOT gate on the output. So, while the NOT gate might not have its own dedicated "node" on the perimeter of your 2D map, it acts as the **axis of symmetry** for the entire structure. The NOT gate is the operation that flips the system across its center point, turning a high potential (Source) into a true void (Ground).

In a 3D geometric model, the NOT operation is like looking in a mirror or traversing the Space Diagonal of the cube—it takes you to the exact opposite coordinate.
