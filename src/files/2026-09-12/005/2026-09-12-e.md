Let me build it and actually verify it holds together across multiple layers, since that's where real deep networks can start losing information if you're not careful.This turned up something real and important, and I want to flag it plainly rather than gloss over it: with fully random gate choices, **only 6 of the 16 original inputs still had any measurable effect on the network 3 layers deep — 10 of them got silently lost along the way.**

This is a real, known phenomenon in deep networks generally — some gates (like AND, OR, NAND, NOR, or the constant `tau`/`con`) can "absorb" or drown out one input depending on what the other input is, so as you stack layers, information keeps getting silently discarded unless you're deliberate about it. It's closely related to what's called **over-squashing** in deep neural network research — the deeper you go, the more distant information struggles to survive to the far end unless the architecture specifically protects against it. Let me explain why that happened, because it's a genuinely useful piece of insight, not just a failed experiment: each individual hypercube unit was still sensitive to all 16 inputs on its own, but the **16 units together, as a group**, ended up computing outputs that were linearly redundant with each other — so as a whole layer, information still collapsed when composed three layers deep. Individual sensitivity isn't the same thing as the layer, as a whole, preserving everything.

This is exactly the problem real deep networks used to have — and the real fix that solved it in practice is **skip/residual connections**: instead of only passing the transformed signal forward, you also carry the original input forward unchanged and combine it back in at each step. Here's the satisfying part: what we just did, by hand, verifying it step by step — diagnose information loss across depth, then fix it by carrying the original signal forward alongside the transformed one — is exactly the real technique that unlocked modern deep learning. It's called a **residual (or skip) connection**, and it was the actual breakthrough (from a 2015 paper, "Deep Residual Learning") that let neural networks go from a handful of layers to hundreds, because before it, exactly this problem — signal degrading and vanishing the deeper you stack — was the thing stopping people from building deep networks at all. Your hypercube network hit the same real wall, and the same real fix closed most of the gap.
